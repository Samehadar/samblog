In short. If the CEO doesn't understand what's going on in engineering, the cure is transparency, not persuasion. Once a week, a single report across all projects: status, metrics, an explanation and a plan for the troubled ones. Green projects get a line, yellow a paragraph, red the full detail. Once the CEO starts to trust you, they'll stop reading it every week of their own accord.

Principle: how often you report depends on how much the problem is on the recipient's mind

While opaque delivery is the CEO's headache, once a month is not enough. When the topic stops worrying them, the frequency can go down. It's the same “trust follows transparency” logic (section 1), only pointed upwards.

What to do

  1. One set of rules: one set of statuses and one procedure for assigning them across all projects (section 4).
  2. One format: status + metrics + explanation + plan for the troubled projects. One list of projects you can drill into beats three emails.
  3. The ideal form is a dashboard, a link to it and a short text. An email alone, without an explanation of the troubled projects, isn't enough.
  4. Collect the data automatically, so the numbers don't depend on who filled them in.
  5. Send the report every week to the CEO, the COO and their project managers: they are the ones who have to act on red projects. It's worth involving them in designing the statuses themselves too. Bring in other C-level people on request; sales usually have no interest in such a report.
  6. Give more information at the start. Cut it down once trust appears.

Why it works

The report is the main instrument for winning back the CEO's trust. A single format doesn't overload them: in a minute they can see where the problems are, and there's somewhere to drill down for details. And the status rules remove the argument “is this already a problem or not yet”.

Example

The CTO was hired precisely because the CEO couldn't see what was happening in delivery. The company had teams of different kinds: outsourced projects, client implementations, staff augmentation and product development. The working solution: one set of statuses and one format for all units, but each with its own set of metrics (section 4); a weekly dashboard for the CEO and the COO; red projects with an action plan.

Where it breaks

  • A monthly “interesting” report while the problem is on fire: too little and too late.
  • Different formats per unit: the CEO has to assemble the picture themselves.
  • Manual collection: the numbers depend on the mood of whoever fills them in.
  • A report nobody acts on: the CEO saw the red, the PM and the COO never did.
