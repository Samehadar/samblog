In short. Oversee the system the work is done in, not the tasks. First build a process with approval points and walk through it yourself. Then remove the points one by one as you see people handling it. In the end, replace approvals with a budget and a regular review of decisions after the fact.

Model: the oversight ladder

Oversight is constant attention to the system instead of controlling individual tasks. You don't walk around asking “how's it going”; you get the whole picture in one place, at one fixed time — from the process, the metrics and the budget. Freedom is passed to the team in steps. With each step you are less involved, but one function always stays with you — the emergency brake, like the instructor's dual-control pedal: the right to stop the process at any moment.

What to do

  1. Describe the process the way you actually do it, and walk through it yourself before handing it off (section 2).
  2. Work out why the task keeps coming back to you. For each reason, find the tool that lets someone else pick up that part of the work: that's how the criteria get out of your head and onto paper, and everyone starts judging by the same yardstick. The hiring example is below.
  3. Place the control points. There are four kinds:
    • guardrail rules: evaluation criteria, salary bands, limits;
    • approval steps: “nothing moves further without me”;
    • metrics per step, such as the hiring funnel by stage;
    • the emergency brake: the right to stop the process at any moment.
  4. Start with tight control. While there are no people you trust yet, approve almost every step. How many points to place often depends on your own anxiety and the company's stage of growth. But it's better to place them by risk: how experienced the person is in this particular task, whether the decision is reversible, what a mistake costs and how fast it will be noticed. Put a removal date on “reassurance” checks right away. (Amazon: one-way and two-way door decisions; Grove, “High Output Management”)
  5. Remove approvals one at a time, once people's decisions consistently match yours, or almost match but stay within the goals, the rules and the acceptable risk — even if you would have decided differently. The goal is a single final approval.
  6. Replace the final approval with a budget (“N people for X money per quarter”). Watch how it's spent, review the decisions in a one-on-one once a quarter, and expand the budget as confidence grows (section 6).
  7. Look at the system, not at tasks. Once a week, a status per project: a traffic light over several metrics, with intervention only where it turns yellow (section 4).

Why it works

  • An approval is cheap insurance: a mistake is caught before it gets expensive. And it works both ways: the final approval catches not only “too expensive” but also “the team lead turned down a promising candidate” or “offered too little — let's add some”.
  • There's always a bottleneck; the question is which bottleneck to be. Being one at a single approval is far cheaper than being one across the whole process.
  • When you look at the system, you don't need to go to every person. That's what separates oversight from micromanagement.

The ladder says how much to loosen control. The rights map says who decides what. For every type of decision you write down who decides, who approves, who is consulted and who is informed. The level is chosen by how reversible the decision is:

The same ladder — for AI agents

Work is handed to an agent in the same steps: first a human checks every step, then approves only the plan and the result, then the agent works within guardrails — a spending limit, an action log and a human's right to stop it. The one important difference from people: the guardrails are enforced technically, not by agreement.

  • Least privilege: the agent sees and changes only what the task needs.
  • Protected branches and mandatory human review before merging and deploying.
  • Stop points and rollback: when the agent must stop and call a human, and how to undo its changes.
  • A named owner: a specific person is responsible for every agent-driven process and its incidents.

The bottleneck then shifts to verification: code appears faster than it can be reviewed and accepted. Watch the review queue, not just the generation speed. Part of the verification can go to automated quality gates: linters, tests, security and licence checks, an AI reviewer, a cap on change size. I covered how to assemble them — and why you should judge the code, not who wrote it — in “Gatekeeping 2.0”.

Sources: GitHub, risks and mitigations for Copilot; DORA AI Capabilities Model, 2025; DORA, State of AI-assisted software development, 2025.

Where it breaks

  • Handing off and no longer looking at all. The right way: hand off, keep watch and step in when people start to struggle.
  • Keeping the approval of every step forever. You are the bottleneck again, and frequent rejections demotivate.
  • Not replacing a team lead when it's time. Replacing them is normal, and often the cheapest option, when every decision turns into an argument: everything has to be discussed, justified and fought over again, and that eats your time. Before replacing, check two things: whether the goals, the limits and the context are clear to them, and whether you yourself have been reversing your own decisions. And tell the difference: “decides differently from how I would, but within the goals” — that is delegation, not a reason to replace. (Amazon: “disagree and commit”; Lara Hogan, Delegation is an art)
  • Teaching everyone yourself. New team leads can be taught by experienced ones (cross-pollination).
  • Sitting inside a process you handed off. Watching “how the ants run” isn't oversight; it's a return to operations.

Check yourself

How does overseeing the system differ from controlling tasks?

Controlling tasks is going to people and asking how it's going. Overseeing the system is getting the whole picture in one place, at one fixed time: a weekly traffic-light status per project, a hiring-funnel dashboard, the remaining budget. You step in when something turns yellow, not on a round-the-floor schedule.

You approve every offer and have become the bottleneck. What next?

Check whether the team leads' decisions match yours, or fit within the goals and the rules (competency matrix, bands, candidate profile). If yes, a quarterly budget and review after the fact. If not, get the team leads trained — not necessarily by you.

Which function does the leader never hand off at any step?

The emergency brake.
