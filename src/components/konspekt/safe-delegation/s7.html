In short. Your job is to organise the work, not to do it for people. Even in a well-tuned system a share of tasks (10–20% from experience) still lands on you: here the expertise is missing, there a referee is needed. Beyond that, you keep what can't be delegated and what keeps the system alive: current processes, rebuilding for change, and turning leadership's goals into work.

Model: the balance of responsibility

What stays with you

Planning, organising, motivating and controlling — Fayol's four functions — are the leader's core job. More specifically:

  • Strategy and turning leadership's goals into plans — mostly through the same processes.
  • Key people: hiring managers into your org and mentoring those growing into management. Hiring key people is any leader's top priority.
  • Your own learning.
  • Living processes: keeping them current and rebuilding them when the environment changes.
  • Exceptions: tasks that fall out of the system, and the role of referee. Some advise splitting authority among middle managers with a built-in conflict of interest, so that unresolved disputes come to you. It's more reliable to separate responsibilities but bind people with shared goals and explicit decision rights: conflict in teams worsens results on average. Arbitration is a normal part of the role, not the purpose of the structure. (De Dreu, Weingart: conflict and team performance, a meta-analysis)
  • The emergency brake in every process you handed off.

Why this isn't “doing nothing”

If you've built a setup that runs without you for 100 people, you'll be trusted with 300–400. And the world changes fast: a new strategy means new people, a new structure and new processes — and that's your work, which nobody else will do.

A growth path that isn't only through management

Another part of your job is to make sure strong people have a growth path that doesn't force everyone into management.

  • The technical track (IC, individual contributor). Senior → Staff → Principal levels with growing influence and pay without managing people. Then management is for those who want to manage, not those who have nowhere else to grow.
  • The “pendulum”. You can come back from management to engineering and go again without the stigma of failure. Charity Majors describes it as a normal career for a strong engineer: manager and engineer are two parallel tracks, not rungs of one ladder.
  • Sponsorship, not only mentoring. A mentor advises; a sponsor gives a meaningful opportunity — a project, a role, a talk — and the support to handle it.
  • Support after promotion. Coaching, feedback and practice are needed in the new role too: the job doesn't end with the promotion announcement.

As for AI agents: they don't make managers redundant. Supervising agents and checking their work is work too. In 2026 Will Larson argues the opposite of “retreating into strategy”: managers should move back closer to the technology, check changes and be accountable for them. That's a practitioner's opinion, not research, but a telling one.

Sources: Charity Majors, The Engineer/Manager Pendulum; Lara Hogan, on sponsorship; Google re:Work, developing managers; Will Larson, Revised rules of engineering leadership.

Example

A site director came back from a two-week holiday to find nothing had fallen apart and nobody had called him once. And it upset him: “So what am I even here for?” That isn't a reason to worry; it's a sign that the delegation infrastructure works. The freed-up time goes to the tasks in the list above.

Where it breaks

  • Losing the point of the role pulls you back into operations. Every task starts routing through you again.
  • Handing off and no longer looking at all. When people start to struggle, you'll be the last to know.
  • Sitting inside a process you handed off. Then why hand it off at all?

Check yourself

Where is the line between oversight and the ant farm?

Oversight is through metrics, statuses and budgets, with intervention when there are problems. The ant farm is daily watching of how the handed-off process is going.

Why is the leader needed even when everything works?

10–20% of tasks still land on them, the system needs maintenance and rebuilding for change, and nobody else will take on strategy and key people.
